We thank you and the reviewer for the careful reading of our manuscript and for the constructive suggestions.
In response, we substantially expanded the forecast evaluation, clarified the roles of the different data sources, and streamlined the methodological presentation. The principal revisions are summarized below and described in detail in our point-by-point responses.

\section*{Summary of Major Revisions}
\begin{itemize}
\item We rebuilt the forecasting validation around five version-consistent rolling-origin cutoffs and compared the proposed method against simpler Bayesian alternatives and the raw ECMWF/GloFAS and NWS forecast products.
\item We added forecast-window CRPS values and targeted quantile diagnostics; we also aligned the final forecasting protocol with the most recent forecast issuance available at each cutoff.
\item We added a focused analysis to assess the contribution of the selected extended-likelihood multivariate model and clarified how the Bayesian quantile-based correction-and-synthesis framework improves forecasting.
\item The manuscript revisions clarify the roles of observations, retrospective products, and forecast products, tighten the methodological exposition, and expand the computational and reproducibility discussion.
\end{itemize}

Detailed point-by-point responses follow below.

\section{Response to the Handling Editor}

\begin{commentblock}
Handling editor comment:

This paper develops a Bayesian dynamical model approach for river flow forecasting. A reviewer and I have read the manuscript, and although this is an important problem, and the proposed methods are interesting, there are some serious shortcomings of the submitted manuscript that need to be addressed.
\end{commentblock}


\commentheading{\HE{1}}
\begin{commentblock}
- The abstract purports a fast approach to the proposed DQLM framework, but that doesn't seem to be verified anywhere in the manuscript -- what is the computational time and complexity of the proposed method? Once it is fitted, does it need to be reestimated in future months or years? Is it feasible to do so?
\end{commentblock}

\responseheading
\begin{responsebox}

Thank you for raising this point. We now report the computational time and discuss the need for refitting in Section 2.4. For the single-site application, fitting the seven quantile-specific models in parallel and producing the forecasts required approximately two hours on a Linux server with 64 cores and 503 GiB of RAM. Because the available timing records do not reliably separate fitting from forecasting, we report the combined wall-clock time. The model must be refitted as new observations and forecast inputs become available. At the scale considered here, the measured runtime suggests that regular refitting is feasible once the data-processing pipeline has been established.

The available timing records, through the populated \texttt{runtime\_sec\_total} and \texttt{runtime\_sec} fields, support this about two hours end-to-end estimate but do not support a reliable decomposition between model fitting and forecasting/post-processing.

The main computational bottleneck is repeated Kalman filtering and smoothing, with leading-order cost proportional to \((T+H)d^{3}\) per full pass, where \(T\) is the historical length, \(H\) the forecast horizon, and \(d\) the latent-state dimension. However, the quantile-specific models can be fitted in parallel.
\end{responsebox}

\commentheading{\HE{2}}
\begin{commentblock}
- One of the main issues with the paper is that the proposed method is not validated properly -- there are no competing alternatives (despite models, A, B and C being listed), the extended asymmetric Laplace is not really given validation either (how does this compare against just an asymmetric Laplace in this problem setting?). I imagine this hierarchical model is likely so complicated that no working hydrologists would understand, nor use it, without very clear evidence that it was substantially superior to other forecast products. The method should be validated against competing statistical models, as well as physical forecast ensembles to illustrate that the predictive distributions are sharper and better calibrated than simpler alternatives.
\end{commentblock}

\responseheading
\begin{responsebox}
We agree that the original manuscript did not validate the proposed forecasting framework strongly enough. In the revised manuscript, we address that directly through a five-cutoff rolling-origin out-of-sample evaluation. At each cutoff, we generate predictive distributions over the 28-day GloFAS/Bayesian forecast window and verify them against future USGS observations that were not used in model fitting. Because the archived NWS forecasts provide eight valid daily leads for these origins, we report NWS in a separate horizon-matched eight-day comparison.

Constructing this validation required recovering and harmonizing large retrospective archives, ensemble-forecast archives, and forecast covariates across institutions. In the course of this revision, we also corrected the earlier retrospective and forecast-product version-matching issue so that the final comparisons use only version-consistent forecasting folds.

To describe the models considered, we use the compact label \(L\)-\(S\)-\(T\). Here \(L\in\{\mathrm{N},\mathrm{AL},\mathrm{exAL}\}\), where N denotes Normal Dynamic Linear Model (NDLM), AL denotes Dynamic Quantile Linear Model (DQLM) with asymmetric Laplace likelihood, and exAL denotes exDQLM with extended asymmetric Laplace likelihood. The \(N\) rows are Gaussian DLM baselines scored through posterior predictive distributions, not fitted quantile-lane models. Next, \(S\in\{\mathrm{U},\mathrm{M}\}\), where U is univariate (no retrospective products or forecast ensembles from climate institutions) and M is multivariate (uses retrospective products and forecast ensembles from ECMWF/GloFAS and NWS). Finally, \(T\in\{\mathrm{T0},\mathrm{T1}\}\), where T0 means no transfer function during the forecast window and T1 means the transfer function remains active during the forecast window, using forecast precipitation and soil-moisture covariates from NOAA/GEFS.

In addition, we benchmark these Bayesian models against the raw ECMWF/GloFAS product over the 28-day evaluation window and against the raw NWS product in a separate eight-day NWS-horizon comparison.

All Bayesian models and raw forecast products are evaluated on the same transformed discharge scale and under the same preprocessing rules for each forecasting horizon. We use mean forecast-window CRPS as the principal cross-model validation metric and report quantile diagnostics separately.

\medskip
\noindent Table 1 in the revised manuscript reports mean 28-day forecast-window CRPS values by cutoff for the nine Bayesian model families and the raw GloFAS product. NWS is omitted from the 28-day table because the archived NWS products provide eight valid daily leads for these origins. Lower values are better, and the smallest value in each cutoff column is shown in bold.\par
\medskip

\noindent Table 2 in the revised manuscript repeats the comparison on the common eight-day horizon available for NWS, GloFAS, and the Bayesian predictive distributions. This keeps the NWS reference visible without mixing forecast horizons in the 28-day table.\par
\medskip

Together, these tables provide the requested validation against simpler Bayesian alternatives and raw operational forecast products, with each product evaluated over its appropriate forecasting horizon. The main conclusion is that the source-aware multivariate synthesis improves the 28-day GloFAS-horizon comparison by using historical observations, retrospective products, and issued forecast products to produce full posterior predictive distributions rather than relying on a single raw product trajectory. The separate eight-day table keeps the NWS comparison on its available horizon and shows that gains over the raw products and simpler Bayesian alternatives are horizon- and origin-dependent rather than uniform across every comparison.
\end{responsebox}

\responseheading
\begin{responsebox}
We agree that the original manuscript did not clearly show how the selected specification depends on its main structural blocks. We therefore added a component-removal sensitivity analysis of the selected \texttt{exAL-M-T1} model in the Appendix (Tables A1 and A2). Because this is a sensitivity analysis rather than a primary benchmark comparison, the revised manuscript reports it in the appendix. The appendix sensitivity analysis uses the same selected specification, cutoff dates, preprocessing, external inputs, likelihood, forecast horizons, and selected hyperparameters as the main validation comparison, while removing one structural component at a time. This fixed sensitivity design assesses whether the retained trend, transfer-function, and seasonal components are supported without changing the remaining analysis.

More specifically, we compared the following models:
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{exAL-M-T1} (full reference): trend + three retained seasonal harmonic pairs + transfer-function components, with the transfer function active in both fitting and forecasting;
\item \texttt{exAL-M-T1-noTrend}: the full model without the trend state;
\item \texttt{exAL-M-noTF}: the full model without transfer information in either fitting or the forecast window;
\item \texttt{exAL-M-T1-noH1}, \texttt{exAL-M-T1-noH2}, and \texttt{exAL-M-T1-noH3}: the full model with the first, second, or third retained seasonal harmonic pair removed from the seasonal state block. \end{itemize}

\medskip
\noindent Table A1 reports mean 28-day forecast-window CRPS for the full \texttt{exAL-M-T1} model and reduced versions obtained by removing individual components, as well as the raw GloFAS product as the horizon-compatible raw reference. Table A2 repeats the same component-removal comparisons over the common eight-day NWS horizon and includes both raw GloFAS and raw NWS.\par
\medskip

For this study, the full reference model has lower CRPS than each reduced model at all five cutoffs under both horizon contracts. The results provide a component-removal sensitivity check supporting the retained combination of transfer-function, trend, and multi-frequency seasonal structure.
This sensitivity analysis complements the main model comparisons presented in response to HE-2.


\end{responsebox}


\commentheading{\HE{4}}
\begin{commentblock}
- Again, on validation: include (along with comparisons against alternative models) proper scores quantifying the quality of the predictive distributions. CRPS is used in model estimation, apparently, but why are CRPS scores not reported out for the forecasting exercise? And, how do they change depending on regime? Because this is a quantile-based method, it is fair to include quantile scores as well (see Gneiting and Raftery 2007 for details).

\responseheading
\begin{responsebox}
We agree. In the revised manuscript, forecast-window CRPS is now reported explicitly for the out-of-sample forecasting exercise, and we also add targeted quantile check-loss diagnostics. The CRPS comparisons across cutoffs, benchmark models, and raw forecast products are reported in Tables 1 and 2, as explained in our response to HE-2 above.

We use CRPS as the primary forecast-window scoring rule because the object of interest is the full synthesized predictive distribution rather than any single quantile in isolation, and CRPS is an integrated quantile-score criterion. The revised manuscript now distinguishes this forecast-scoring role from the preliminary pre-cutoff calibration of selected specification choices. Following the proper-scoring-rule perspective of Gneiting and Raftery (2007), we complement the full-distribution CRPS summary with quantile check losses at the targeted levels \(\tau\in\{0.05,0.20,0.35,0.50,0.65,0.80,0.95\}\), using USGS observations as verification targets. We include these diagnostics for the four main synthesis competitors \texttt{exAL-M-T1}, \texttt{AL-M-T1}, \texttt{exAL-U-T1}, and \texttt{AL-U-T1}, so the reader can see how quantile-level performance changes across cutoff windows. The results are presented in Table 3 of the revised manuscript.

\medskip
\noindent Table 3 reports mean forecast-window quantile check loss at the targeted quantiles for the four principal synthesis competitors, on the same \(\log(1+Q)\) scale used in the CRPS calculations. Lower values indicate better quantile-level predictive performance, and within each cutoff panel the smallest value in each quantile column (ties included) is shown in bold.\par

The main practical pattern is that the multivariate synthesis models have lower check loss than the univariate references in most cutoff--quantile cells. The \texttt{exAL-M-T1} model is strongest through most of the first three cutoffs, while \texttt{AL-M-T1} is competitive in selected lower-tail or final-cutoff cells. Together with the CRPS comparison summarized in Table 1 and Table 2 and explained in HE-2, these diagnostics show how forecast performance varies across the targeted quantile levels and across the rolling-origin cutoff windows.

\end{responsebox}

\commentheading{\HE{6}}
\begin{commentblock}
- Something that wasn't clear to me in the application was: is this in-sample, or out-of-sample validation? It appears that the model was fit to the data that is being used in validation, which isn't realistic. Please perform an appropriate cross-validation study to investigate the approach's actual ability to forecast unseen data.
\end{commentblock}

\responseheading
\begin{responsebox}
We agree that this needed to be clarified. The revised forecasting evaluation is out-of-sample and uses five cutoff-specific, rolling-origin forecast-window evaluations.

For each cutoff date \(\textbf{c}\), we construct a version-consistent staged dataset. The model is fit using only observations and retrospective information available through \(\textbf{c}\): observed discharge up to \(\textbf{c}\) and retrospective/historical products available at \(\textbf{c}\). Forecasts are then generated over the post-cutoff forecast window using the latest forecast products issued at or before \(\textbf{c}\), together with the forecast-window precipitation and soil-moisture covariates staged in the same origin bundle. The quantile-based analysis requires seven quantile-specific fits at each cutoff, followed by posterior predictive synthesis before CRPS scoring. Post-cutoff USGS observations remain verification only and are not used to fit or update the predictive distributions. The canonical GDPC climate-index covariate is sliced consistently to the cutoff-specific bundle; it is not treated as an operational forecast product or as a verification target.

As noted in HE-2, the five cutoffs were retained only when the forecast inputs, retrospective products, and forecast covariates were all archive-feasible and version-consistent. The evaluation folds were therefore determined by timing and data availability, not by forecast performance.

\end{responsebox}


\commentheading{\HE{7}}
\begin{commentblock}
- The weighted combination of prior forecasts at differing horizons is curious to me. Presumably the most recent forecasts should be better with more available atmospheric data, whereas longer lead-time forecasts likely exhibit more bias. How does the model perform if you only use the most recent forecast (which I would expect to be the highest quality)?
\end{commentblock}


\responseheading
\begin{responsebox}
We agree that, from an operational forecasting perspective, the most recent forecast is the natural reference.

We therefore repeated the analysis without combining forecasts issued at different times. For GloFAS, we use the daily forecast issued on the cutoff date. For NWS, we consider only information available by the cutoff and retain the most recent issuance for each target date and ensemble member before aggregating the forecasts to daily values. The revised manuscript uses this procedure throughout the forecasting analysis.
This implements the latest available forecast ensemble rather than combining earlier issuances. The reported publication matrices do not average older issuances, even where historical workflow file names retain older descriptive labels. Under this procedure, \texttt{exAL-M-T1} has the lowest 28-day CRPS in all five rolling-origin cutoffs. The 28-day comparison includes the raw GloFAS product but not raw NWS because the available NWS forecasts cover only eight days. We therefore report a separate comparison in which all forecasts are evaluated
over the common eight-day horizon.

\end{responsebox}


\section{Response to Reviewer 1}

\begin{commentblock}

Reviewer 1 Comments to the Author

This paper develops a Bayesian quantile regression approach, based on asymmetric Laplace distributions, to predict and forecast streamflow in the Santa Cruz region of the US. The work appears strong in the development of the Bayesian models and the novelty in the extension of inference methods is accepted. However, the improvement of forecasts through dynamical error correction is a common strategy and the novelty of this part may need to be elucidated further. The paper is framed as a forecasting paper, however the forecasting component is limited and robust forecast evaluation is absent. I recommend that some substantial revisions, mainly structural, are required before proceeding with publication. My comments below don’t cover everything, but are intended as a push into reshaping:
\end{commentblock}
\commentheading{\RoneO}
\responseheading
\begin{responsebox}
We agree that, in its original form, the manuscript gave too much emphasis to model development and not enough to forecast validation. We have substantially revised the application to include five out-of-sample forecast periods, comparisons with simpler Bayesian models and raw GloFAS and NWS forecasts, and evaluations based on CRPS and quantile-specific check loss. We also revised the analysis to use only the most recent forecast ensemble available at each forecast date.


We have also clarified the methodological contribution. We do not claim that dynamic forecast correction is itself new. The proposed framework links observations, retrospective products, and operational forecast ensembles through a shared latent quantile process while learning source-specific, time-varying discrepancies. It carries source corrections learned from retrospective products into the forecast-period source structure, combines the quantile-specific forecasts into a single predictive distribution, and uses a variational Bayes procedure based on Laplace--Delta approximations for the extended dynamic quantile linear model.

The revised manuscript now does this by centering the forecasting analysis on multiple rolling-origin cutoffs, comparing the proposed method against simpler Bayesian alternatives and raw forecast products, reporting both CRPS and targeted quantile diagnostics, and presenting the contribution as a correction-and-synthesis framework supported by rolling-origin forecast evaluation and selected-model interpretation rather than treating dynamic discrepancy correction alone as the central novelty.
\end{responsebox}

\commentheading{\RoneM{5}}

5. A robust discussion of how the method can be set up for fair forecast assessment using cross-validation might be required.

\responseheading
\begin{responsebox}
We agree that fair assessment of this method must be organized around forecast origins rather than a conventional random split or a single split of the time series. The revised analysis uses five cutoff-specific forecast-window evaluations. At each forecast date, only information available at that time is used to fit the quantile-specific models and synthesize the posterior predictive distribution, while subsequent post-cutoff USGS observations are used only for verification. A larger number of forecast dates would be useful in a future study, but each retained origin requires recovering a version-consistent bundle of observations, retrospective products, issued forecast products, and forecast-window covariates from evolving gridded archives, followed by seven quantile-specific fits and posterior predictive synthesis. We therefore use archive-feasible, version-consistent origins that span contrasting hydrological settings and avoid dense overlapping windows that would repeatedly score the same hydrological regime.

The revised manuscript also clarifies the preliminary calibration of the transfer-persistence parameter \(\lambda\). This calibration is not a nested forecast-window tuning step: component discount factors were held fixed, candidate \(\lambda\) values were compared for the median-quantile model using CRPS over the pre-cutoff observational window at the December 25, 2022 origin, and the selected value \(\lambda=0.97\) was then held fixed for the reported forecast-window evaluations. The forecast-window CRPS tables therefore evaluate a fixed calibrated specification against held-out future USGS observations.
\end{responsebox}
